Nifty FMCG: building the index, then a portfolio to beat it

Five stocks passed the valuation and ratio screens; allocated a ₹10 cr model fund Open caseClose case 10constituents, rebuilt as a market-cap index from base 1,000 51.3average sector P/E used as the dividing line 5 of 10stocks accepted into the portfolio

What I built

  • A market-cap-weighted FMCG index of the ten largest constituents, rebased to 1,000 on 21 May 2026 and recalculated daily from price moves and share counts.
  • A two-sided valuation screen around the 51.3 average P/E. Below average: accepted if revenue and profit were both rising. Above average: accepted only if PEG was under 1.5 and positive. All five high-P/E names failed (Nestlé PEG 6.07, Godrej Consumer 9.4).
  • A rank on six ratios (current, quick, debt/equity, interest cover, ROE, profit margin) to decide weights.
  • A ₹10 cr model fund with NAV starting at ₹10, tracked daily against my index.

The tracking result, plainly

By 25 June 2026 the fund's NAV was ₹9.65 and my index 982.2, so the portfolio fell 3.5% while the index fell 1.8%. Five weeks is too short to judge a stock screen, but the gap shows how much a 35% weight in a single name drives short-run results.

The filters

Each company was scored on P/E, PEG (≤1.25), P/BV, debt/equity (≤0.5), gross and operating margin, interest cover, ROE and FCF/sales (≥10%), with a written note on its moat. Every rejection records the specific test it failed, so the screen can be audited line by line.

Most rejections came from cash conversion rather than valuation: many well-known names with strong margins still turned less than a tenth of sales into free cash.

Finoability internship, 2026

Indian banks: separate yardsticks for private and public-sector lenders

17 of 32 banks accepted on nine banking-specific ratios Open caseClose case 11 of 20private banks accepted 6 of 12public-sector banks accepted 11.5%RBI minimum CAR used as the floor

Method

Built a parameter sheet first: NIM, cost-to-income, gross NPA, credit growth, CASA, ROA, ROE, capital adequacy and P/BV, each with good, acceptable and poor bands. NIM gets different thresholds for private (above 3.5% is good) and PSU banks (above 2.5%), because their funding models differ.

Every accept or reject carries a one-line reason, for example Bank of Maharashtra accepted on the highest PSU ROA (1.64%) and ROE (21.11%); IndusInd rejected on 3.43% GNPA and 0.16% ROA.

Finoability internship, 2026. Valuation-timing backtest

Nifty Next 50: does buying on P/E signals beat buying and holding?

₹1 lakh in 1999 grows to ₹8.53 cr on P/E timing, against about ₹39 lakh held throughout Open caseClose case 27 yearsof daily index closes, Jan 1999 to Mar 2026 5buy-sell cycles 872%best single cycle, Sep 2001 to Oct 2007

The setup

₹1,00,000 invested in the Nifty Next 50 on the buy and sell dates signalled by the Nifty 50 P/E study. Between cycles the money earns fixed-deposit interest at the prevailing rate (8%, then 6.5%, then 5.7% a year), compounded daily with FV() formulas.

What it shows

Buying near valuation troughs (Sep 2001, Oct 2008, Aug 2013, Mar 2020) and selling near peaks compounds far faster than holding: about ₹8.53 cr against roughly ₹39.4 lakh on the same index. The last exit in August 2020 has sat in deposits since, which is the cost of a strict rule.

This is a backtest with the benefit of hindsight on the thresholds, so I read it as evidence that valuation matters for entry, not as a return forecast.

Process

  • A scored risk questionnaire covering age, capacity for loss, market familiarity, experience, preference and reaction to drawdowns.

WeSchool coursework, 2026. Credit risk analysis

Ashok Leyland: should a bank lend, and on what terms?

Approve up to ₹2,000 cr, unsecured, up to 5 years Open caseClose case ₹5,856 crnet cash, FY26 (standalone) 0.26x → 0.09xdebt / equity, FY24 to FY26 33.8xinterest cover on reported finance cost AA+ / A1+ICRA view, broadly matching mine

The question

An independent FY24–FY26 credit view of India's second-largest M&HCV maker, built from three annual reports before reading any rating agency report, ending in a lending decision with covenants.

How I worked

  • Assessed the standalone company: consolidated debt of ~₹63,650 cr is mostly the NBFC arm's lending book and would show leverage at 3.4x equity against 0.09x for the vehicle business. Group risk was captured separately through guarantees and investments.
  • Made three adjustments that change the picture: excluded other income from operating EBITDA; stripped a ₹1,453 cr dividend-account timing swing out of cash flow; counted cash net of earmarked balances.
  • Reconciled total debt to the company's own capital-management note for all three years (₹2,299 / 1,482 / 1,195 cr) to confirm the extraction.
  • Stress-tested an FY20-style downturn: volumes down 30%, EBITDA margin at 8%, ₹1,000 cr of payables unwinding while capex and dividends continue. Interest cover stays above 25x and the company stays near net cash for about two years.

The call

A strong credit at the top of the high-safety band, stable outlook. Approve unsecured facilities with covenants: net debt/EBITDA ≤1.5x; debt plus guarantees/net worth ≤0.5x; a cap on new funding to subsidiaries; no inter-corporate deposits to group firms outside the vehicle business; timely completion of the HLF merger.

The one number that would change my view: cash less supplier dues (−₹1,278 cr today). Past −₹3,000 cr in a falling market, I would move to a secured, shorter-tenor structure.

What the rating agency taught me

Read only after my view was final. ICRA's AA+ (Stable) sits in the same band, and its FY25 interest cover of 22.7x matched mine exactly. The real gap: I weighted past capex, while ICRA weighted about ₹1,800 cr a year of planned capex and EV investment. That is the stronger argument against an AAA-equivalent view, and I accepted it. It did not change the lending decision, but it made the subsidiary-funding cap essential rather than sensible.

Deliverables: five-sheet Excel model (~40 ratios as live formulas with annual-report page references), credit report, credit committee sheet, CRA comparison, and a five-action rating-resilience plan.

WeSchool coursework, 2026. Fund-raising & market risk

DMart: how should it fund ₹1,000 crore of new stores?

Raise it through AAA-rated NCDs, 7.25–7.60% fixed, in 3- and 5-year tranches Open caseClose case −₹647 crfree cash flow, FY26, after −₹960 cr in FY25 0.14xgearing after the bond, against a 0.20x downgrade trigger 4.30NCD weighted score, highest of four routes

The question

DMart buys the land and builds every store, so growth is capital-hungry. In FY26 operating cash of ₹3,467 cr fell short of ₹4,113 cr capex, the second deficit in a row. I compared four ways to raise about ₹1,000 cr and assessed how lenders and CRISIL would read the result.

Why not the cheapest option

Commercial paper was cheapest at 6.40–7.18%, but it lasts 90 days. DMart's own filings show it repaid ₹300 cr on 7 September 2026 and reissued ₹300 cr the next day. Funding a 20-year store that way means repeating that rollover about 80 times. Equity (a QIP) would cost roughly twice the bond for a 0.42% dilution, with the share price near its 52-week low.

Where the risk actually sits

Not in the balance sheet. EBITDA margin slipped from 8.08% to 7.54%, like-for-like growth halved to 8.1%, revenue per sq ft fell for the first time, and DMart Ready lost ₹307 cr in FY26 while retreating from 24 cities to 11.

Conditions I attached

  • Size the issue to the actual funding gap, not the available capacity.
  • Stagger tranches so the coupon isn't set at one point on the curve.
  • Use part of the proceeds to retire the ₹380 cr overdraft and end the CP rollover.
  • Hold gearing below 0.15x, keeping a buffer under CRISIL's 0.20x trigger.

The investment banker's role

Plain senior unsecured NCD, listed on BSE, privately placed. Priced off the G-Sec curve (~6.96%) at 40–70 bp over. Insurers and pension funds anchor the 5-year tranche; mutual fund corporate-bond schemes take the 3-year. Existing equity holders' debt desks are warm introductions.

Deliverables: five-sheet Excel (snapshot, ratio analysis, weighted funding matrix, risk dashboard, recommendation), funding-route comparison, market and rating risk assessment, and a short note.

WeSchool live project. Business analysis

Private equity in India: how would a new fund break in?

Enter as a Tier-2 healthcare roll-up platform, not a blind-pool fund Open caseClose case 10global and domestic firms benchmarked 4 of 5Porter's forces rated high 2target corridors, West and South

Research

  • Mapped India AUM and share for Blackstone, KKR, Carlyle, TPG and Apollo against ChrysCapital, Kedaara, Everstone, True North and Multiples.
  • Traced regulation from SEBI's 1996 VCF rules through the 2012 AIF regulations, pass-through tax status and the 2024 valuation circular, alongside Dodd-Frank, AIFMD and ERISA.
  • Built strategy canvases on capital size, sector expertise, operating support, governance and networks, cross-checked with a primary interview: team quality was ranked above track record.
  • Porter's five forces: rivalry, buyer (LP) power, supplier (promoter) power and substitutes all high; new entrants low to moderate.

The entry strategy

A first-time manager can't raise a blind pool without a track record, and large funds crowd Tier-1 auctions. So: acquire a profitable Tier-2 anchor hospital or diagnostics chain in the Maharashtra–Gujarat or Karnataka–Tamil Nadu corridor, roll up smaller clinics and labs, centralise procurement and billing, and fund deal by deal through SPVs with co-investment rights.

LPs back visible assets and live cash flows rather than a pitch. Acquire, integrate, show results, raise, repeat.
